Every row is one price level of the order book. Green bars on the lower half are bids (buyers), red bars on the upper half are asks (sellers), and the bar length is how many shares are queued there. Trading trajectories

What am I looking at?

Shares still to buy, against time. TWAP is the straight line: trade the same amount every minute. The Almgren–Chriss curve bends below it, and the more urgent you are (higher λ) the harder it bends — you pay more impact to spend less time exposed to the price moving against you. Push λ to the bottom of its range and the curve becomes the straight line: a trader who does not care about risk should trade uniformly, and the model says so.

Cost decomposition closed form

What am I looking at?

Where the expected cost comes from, in basis points of the order's value. Permanent is the price move your order leaves behind — it depends on how much you trade, never on how you slice it, so it is the same bar for every algorithm. Spread is the half-spread and fees paid on each share. Temporary is the extra you pay for trading fast, and it is the only part any schedule can change. The line marks one standard deviation of the total cost: notice that it falls as the temporary cost rises. That trade is the entire subject of the next tab.

Efficient frontier live + simulated points

What am I looking at?

Each point on the line is the best you can do at some level of tolerance for cost uncertainty: move right and you accept a wider range of outcomes in exchange for a lower expected cost. No strategy exists below and to the left of the line, and any strategy above it is being beaten by one on it. The markers are what the same schedules actually cost when run through the microstructure simulator, so the vertical gap between a marker and the line is the model's error — the honest measure of how much the closed form can be trusted.

Urgency, in units a trader uses

What am I looking at?

λ is in inverse dollars and nobody has intuition for it. The useful translation is the half-life — the time by which the order is half finished. The table shows the same frontier read as: pick how quickly you want to be half done, and this is the expected cost and cost dispersion that choice implies.

Distribution of implementation shortfall

What am I looking at?

One histogram per algorithm, over identical simulated sessions — every algorithm saw the same order flow and the same price path, which is what makes the comparison fair. Positive is cost. The distributions overlap heavily, and that is the point: the spread of outcomes on a single order dwarfs the difference between algorithms, which is why execution quality is judged over hundreds of orders and never over one.

Impact measured from the simulator precomputed

What am I looking at?

The Almgren–Chriss cost model needs two numbers: how far a trade moves the price permanently, and how much extra it costs to trade quickly. Neither was assumed. Each was measured by running parent orders through the simulator and comparing against the identical session with no order in it — a counterfactual, which is the only way to separate a price move you caused from one that was going to happen anyway. Left: permanent impact against order size, with the fitted slope. Right: the cost of speed against trading rate, on log axes, with the linear model the closed form assumes.

What am I looking at?

Each name's simulated session is checked against the daily data it was calibrated to: does it print the right daily volume, and does its mid price move with the right volatility? The last two columns are what the simulator produced but nobody targeted — the spread and the depth at the touch emerge from the order flow model rather than being set, and they land in the range these names actually quote.

What free daily data cannot tell you about the spread

What am I looking at?

Two published estimators that recover an effective spread from daily high/low/close bars, applied to these eight names, against the one-tick spread these names actually quote. Both are implemented correctly — the test suite recovers a known spread from a synthetic Roll model with both — and both are useless here, disagreeing with the truth and with each other by one to two orders of magnitude. The reason is structural: they assume the daily range is dominated by the spread, and for a name with a 90 bp daily range and a 0.13 bp spread it is dominated by volatility instead. So this project does not take its spread from daily bars. It seeds the book at one tick and lets the order flow decide, and this chart is here as the reason rather than being quietly omitted.

What am I looking at?

The same five algorithms, simulated here and now against the model's own price and cost dynamics rather than against the order book. It takes a few hundred milliseconds, which is exactly why this can respond to a slider while the book simulation cannot. Compare the numbers with the precomputed ones on the cost tab: where they agree, the closed form is a fair description of the venue; where they disagree, the microstructure is doing something the model has no way to express — a finite book that runs out, and clustered flow that arrives when it wants to rather than when the schedule wants it.

Where the venue stops absorbing the order precomputed

What am I looking at?

Fill rate and cost against parent size, from the full order book simulation. Up to about one percent of a day's volume in half an hour, nearly everything completes and costs grow roughly linearly. Past that the book cannot supply the shares inside the horizon: the fill rate falls, the cost curve bends upward, and the unfilled remainder gets charged at whatever the price did — which is the cost that closed-form models, all of which assume the order can always be executed, simply do not have a term for.
